Beyond Vanilla SSMs

Is there an alternative to transformer networks? A look at where state-space models are heading — and what they change about long-context modelling.

AS
Alessio Sampieri
Lead Researcher · 8 min read

For most of the last decade, the transformer has been the default answer to almost every sequence-modelling question. Its self-attention mechanism is expressive and highly parallelisable — but it carries a well-known cost: compute and memory that grow quadratically with sequence length.

The quadratic wall

As context windows stretch from thousands to millions of tokens, that quadratic term stops being an inconvenience and becomes the binding constraint. State-space models (SSMs) offer a different bargain: linear-time sequence processing with a recurrent formulation that can still be trained in parallel.

The question is no longer whether attention works — it clearly does — but whether we always need to pay its price.

What Mamba changes

Selective SSMs make the state transition input-dependent, letting the model choose what to remember and what to discard as it scans a sequence. This recovers much of the content-based reasoning that made attention powerful, while keeping the linear-time profile that makes long context affordable.

In our own experiments across long-document and code settings, hybrid designs — a few attention layers interleaved with selective SSM blocks — consistently gave the best accuracy-per-FLOP. The practical takeaway is less "attention vs. SSM" and more "attention where it earns its cost."

Where this goes next

The frontier is moving toward architectures that adapt their own compute to the difficulty of the input. SSMs are one ingredient in that shift — not a wholesale replacement for the transformer, but a way to make very long context tractable enough to be worth building products around.
